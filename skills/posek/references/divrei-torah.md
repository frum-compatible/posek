# Divrei Torah

Prepare something the person can actually say, learn, or teach. A worthwhile dvar Torah leaves the listener with a clearer reading, a useful distinction, or a specific avodah that follows from the sources. Substance comes from the learning and the argument.

## Establish the brief

Use the supplied parsha or topic, occasion, audience, length, and source preferences. Ask only for a missing fact that would materially change the piece. Otherwise choose a reasonable scope and proceed. For an unspecified Shabbos-table vort, default to one accessible thought in roughly 300–400 spoken words, with a short source list outside the spoken text. Treat speaking time as approximate; quotations, explanations, and pauses take time. A stated word limit overrides this default.

If asked for this week's parsha or an upcoming Yom Tov, verify the date and relevant calendar, including Israel versus chutz la’aretz where readings differ. Do not infer the calendar from the persona, a surname, or a presumed location. A request naming a parsha does not require a calendar lookup.

Distinguish the deliverables:

- **A vort or derashah:** give a finished piece in natural prose, ready to say aloud.
- **A shiur outline:** provide the argument's sequence, source references, and questions that advance the learning.
- **A source sheet:** arrange exact sources in teaching order, with brief framing questions. Distinguish primary text, commentary, translation, and your notes. Do not copy a full modern translation without the necessary rights.
- **A revision:** improve the supplied piece, preserving its sound central idea and requested form. Do not replace it with an unrelated thought just because that is easier.

Do not attach all four deliverables to every request.

## Learn the sources before drafting

Apply [source-method.md](source-method.md). Read the pesukim in their narrative or legal context and the actual peirush on which the thought depends. For a Gemara or Midrash, read enough of the discussion to know who is speaking, whether the statement is challenged, and what the passage concludes. A familiar quotation is a lead until verified.

Choose the few sources needed for one clear thought. A pasuk and one carefully read Rashi can support a complete vort. Add a second mefaresh when it advances the question or provides a necessary distinction. Do not insert a Maharal, Ramban, or gadol's name to make the piece sound more learned.

For each source, identify the proposition it actually establishes. Then keep three layers clear:

1. **The source:** what the pasuk, Chazal, or mefaresh says, with a faithful quotation or paraphrase.
2. **The reading:** why those words or that sequence support the explanation being offered.
3. **The application:** a further inference or contemporary avodah suggested by that reading.

Those layers should be legible in the prose without turning the finished vort into a labeled analysis table. Do not present derash as the only pshat, one reported shittah as unanimous agreement, or a modern application as an explicit statement of Chazal.

## Develop one point worth saying

Begin from a genuine textual question, an unexpected emphasis, a tension between sources, or a precise observation. Before calling something a kushya, check the plain context and grammar. Two different words are not automatically contradictory. If the obvious reading answers the question, use a better question or begin with the observation itself.

Work toward a claim that can be explained in a sentence and supported step by step. A useful progression is the precise detail, the interpretive difficulty, the explanation, and its consequence. This is a reasoning aid, not a required four-paragraph template.

Test the proposed reading against its strongest nearby objection. Read the adjacent passage if it may qualify the conclusion. Do not hide a relevant alternative in Rashi or an inconvenient condition in the Rambam. A short piece can focus on one approach while saying whose approach it is.

An original chiddush is welcome when the steps hold. Introduce the inference honestly: “One way to understand this is…” or “Perhaps we can suggest…”. Do not attach it to a mefaresh who did not say it, and do not claim that nobody has ever proposed it. If the connection is too weak, discard it. More sources cannot repair an invalid inference.

The ending must be earned. It may resolve the opening question, sharpen a distinction, or identify a concrete avodah. It need not become a life lesson. When making an application, show why this source leads to this application; do not turn the pasuk into a pretext for an unrelated message.

## Write and revise

Read [torah-writing.md](torah-writing.md) before delivering or revising the piece. Use those tests privately. The reader should receive the dvar Torah, compact mareh mekomos, and any material source limitation—not your drafting checklist.

Keep spoken content separate from source notes when length matters. Use a title only if it helps; make it specific to the thought. Honor the requested language, structure, and level. Use yeshivish terms naturally with a yeshivish audience; explain them when the audience needs it.

In yeshivish English, use Hebrew script for short source quotations and English for the explanation, following the mixed-language guidance in [torah-writing.md](torah-writing.md). Apply its Anti-Slop editing pass before delivery, including the no-em-dash default for a short vort. The finished piece should sound natural at the table. Keep only source qualifications that matter to the reading or a practical implication.

For an existing draft:

- Identify its actual claim and preserve what works.
- Check quotations, attributions, and the source-to-conclusion links before polishing sentences.
- Repair the argument, remove repetition, and sharpen the ending. Adding learned vocabulary does not fix missing substance.
- If a false quotation, attribution, or halachic conclusion was removed, state that correction briefly outside the finished piece. Do not quietly preserve it for the sake of the author's voice.
- Give the revised text, then only the material changes the user needs to understand. If they requested an outline or line edits instead, honor that format.

## Stories, attribution, and limits

Use a gadol story or quotation only when its provenance can be checked. Do not manufacture a sefer, page number, eyewitness, or “well-known” attribution. A family story supplied by the user may remain their account; do not embellish it with invented dialogue or present it as a verified historical source. Omit a questionable story if the thought stands without it.

Do not use a vort to issue an unearned heter, establish personal status, or advise dangerous conduct. Separate any practical shailah for halachic analysis. In a hesped or a piece for someone suffering, avoid speculation about why the tragedy happened and invented claims about the person's life.

When retrieval is unavailable, work from supplied passages and identify them as supplied. You can outline a direction with clearly marked sources to verify, but do not present an unverified quotation or attribution as checked. If a user asks for a completed sourced piece and essential evidence is unavailable, give the useful portion and name the precise missing source.
