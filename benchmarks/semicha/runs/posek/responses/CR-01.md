**(a) Poultry cooked with milk: the Rishonim and the Gemara**

The three prohibitions—**bishul**, **achilah**, and **hana’ah**—must be distinguished. Chullin 115b derives separate prohibitions of cooking, eating, and benefiting from the three occurrences of לא תבשל. The question is whether poultry belongs to that Torah prohibition, and, if not, how far the rabbinic decree extends. [Chullin 115b:6](https://www.sefaria.org/Chullin.115b.6)

In the Mishnah, **Rabbi Akiva** excludes חיה ועוף from the Torah prohibition. **Rabbi Yosi HaGelili** excludes poultry because it has no mother’s milk. Chullin 116a explains that Rabbi Akiva nevertheless prohibits poultry with milk rabbinically, whereas Rabbi Yosi HaGelili permits it even rabbinically; the latter’s locality actually ate poultry with milk. Rav Ashi, at 104a, explains the opening Mishnah according to Rabbi Akiva: its general prohibition includes both Torah and rabbinic cases. [Chullin 113a:18–19](https://www.sefaria.org/Chullin.113a.18-19), [116a:11–14](https://www.sefaria.org/Chullin.116a.11-14), [104a:7](https://www.sefaria.org/Chullin.104a.7)

**Rambam, Ma’achalot Assurot 9:4**, rules that poultry and חיה with milk are forbidden **to eat only mid’rabbanan**, while cooking and benefit are permitted. **Rosh, Chullin 8:51**, understands Rif to rule like Rabbi Akiva. He explains that the Mishnah’s first tanna need not disagree: Rabbi Akiva supplies the derivation for the exclusions. This avoids treating Rabbi Akiva as a lone opinion against a majority. [Rambam 9:4](https://www.sefaria.org/Mishneh_Torah%2C_Forbidden_Foods.9.4), [Rosh 8:51](https://www.sefaria.org/Rosh_on_Chullin.8.51)

The apparent objection to Rambam is that the Mishnah says all meat is forbidden **“to cook”** in milk. **Maggid Mishneh ad loc.** explains that here the expression concerns cooking for consumption: the rabbinic extension prohibits eating, without a separate prohibition of cooking or benefit. He expressly considers, but does not adopt as Rambam’s view, extending the decree to cooking lest one eat. [Maggid Mishneh, 9:4](https://www.sefaria.org/Maggid_Mishneh_on_Mishneh_Torah%2C_Forbidden_Foods.9.4)

**Tosafot, Chullin 113a, s.v. בשר בהמה טהורה**, read the Rabbis as including poultry in the Torah prohibition, unlike Rabbi Akiva. At **104b, s.v. עוף וגבינה אין**, Tosafot suggest that the prevailing stringency after poultry may reflect not accepting the position that poultry with milk is merely rabbinic. On that Torah-prohibition position, cooking and benefiting are prohibited as well. However, that suggestion should not be presented as Tosafot’s unqualified, independently established final ruling: their discussion offers several explanations. **Shach YD 87:4** explicitly warns that Tosafot’s explanation of the eating practice does not compel a ruling that poultry with milk is de’oraita. [Tosafot 113a:18:1](https://www.sefaria.org/Tosafot_on_Chullin.113a.18.1), [Tosafot 104b:12:1](https://www.sefaria.org/Tosafot_on_Chullin.104b.12.1), [Shach 87:4](https://www.sefaria.org/Siftei_Kohen_on_Shulchan_Arukh%2C_Yoreh_De%27ah.87.4)

The **Mechaber’s ruling, YD 87:3**, is explicit concerning poultry and חיה: **“מותר בבישול ובהנאה”**—cooking and benefit are permitted; eating is rabbinically prohibited. This is permission under the laws of basar b’chalav, not permission to eat the mixture. [Shulchan Arukh, YD 87:3](https://www.sefaria.org/Shulchan_Arukh%2C_Yoreh_De%27ah.87.3)

**(b) Agra: Tosafot, Rambam, and Beit Yosef YD 89**

Agra teaches that poultry and cheese may be eaten **באפיקורן**, explained as **“בלא נטילת ידים ובלא קינוח הפה”**: without washing the hands or cleaning the mouth between them. This concerns eating them successively, not together. The next episode is crucial: Rav Yitzchak ate cheese and then meat without washing his hands; challenged from Agra’s apparent restriction to poultry, he answered that by daylight he could inspect his hands. [Chullin 104b:11–13](https://www.sefaria.org/Chullin.104b.11-13)

**Tosafot’s initial explanation:** Agra’s poultry leniency includes **poultry first, cheese afterward**. Why, then, does it challenge Rav Yitzchak, who ate in the opposite order? Because Agra implies that ordinary meat requires washing and mouth-cleaning. On the interpretation requiring an interval after meat, those measures alone cannot suffice when meat comes first; therefore the implied requirement necessarily includes **cheese first, meat afterward**. The Gemara’s shorthand “meat and cheese” need not preserve the actual order in that inference.

**Rabbeinu Tam and Behag within Tosafot:** they interpret the prohibition of cheese after meat as applying without washing and mouth-cleaning; those measures can permit immediate succession. Mar Ukva’s waiting until another meal was either without those measures or a personal stringency. Tosafot then refine this explanation to account for Rav Yitzchak’s challenge: handwashing is relevant in either order, whereas the distinction between orders concerns mouth-cleaning. Rabbeinu Tam explains poultry’s special treatment by its not adhering to the hands, teeth, and gums.

**Practice recorded by Tosafot:** people nevertheless refrain from cheese immediately after meat, including poultry. Tosafot explore whether Agra follows Rabbi Akiva, why he mentions poultry rather than חיה, and whether another tannaitic position explains him. One answer is that poultry is more common and חיה shares its rule. Thus Tosafot distinguish the scope of Agra’s teaching from the stricter practice they record. All these explanations occur in the same dibbur. [Tosafot, Chullin 104b, s.v. עוף וגבינה אין](https://www.sefaria.org/Tosafot_on_Chullin.104b.12.1)

**Rambam’s conclusion is narrower.** In **9:26–27**, after cheese or milk, poultry requires neither handwashing nor mouth-cleaning. Ordinary meat and חיה require the prescribed cleaning. In **9:28**, however, someone who ate poultry first must wait approximately six hours before milk, just as after ordinary meat, because meat remains between the teeth. Consequently, his reading of Agra applies only to **cheese → poultry**, although he regards poultry with milk as rabbinic. [Rambam 9:26–28](https://www.sefaria.org/Mishneh_Torah%2C_Forbidden_Foods.9.26-28)

**Beit Yosef YD 89, opening comment beginning אכל בשר**, identifies two separate disagreements:

- **Order:** Rambam restricts Agra to cheese first; Ramban, as reported there through Rashba and Ran, permits either order.
- **Species:** Ran extends the leniency to חיה because its milk prohibition is also rabbinic. Rambam treats חיה like בהמה here; Ran suggests that their similar meat explains this. Rashba rejects cheese immediately after poultry or חיה, but permits poultry or חיה after cheese without these cleaning requirements.

Beit Yosef therefore explains why Tur attributes the statement specifically to **Rambam**, rather than simply quoting it as undisputed Gemara: Rambam excludes both the broader reading about order and the broader reading about species. [Beit Yosef, YD 89, opening comment](https://www.sefaria.org/Beit_Yosef%2C_Yoreh_De%27ah.89.1)

The Mechaber follows these distinctions in **YD 89:1–2**: waiting after poultry, but no required קינוח or נטילה before poultry following cheese. Rema’s gloss in 89:2 adds the separate stringent practice after **hard cheese**, including before poultry. [Shulchan Arukh and Rema, YD 89:1–2](https://www.sefaria.org/Shulchan_Arukh%2C_Yoreh_De%27ah.89.1-2)

**(c) Cooking meat for a non-Jew in a dairy pot that is not ben-yomo**

Assume a **clean pot**, with no actual dairy residue, whose relevant dairy absorption is over twenty-four hours old. Cooking for a non-Jew does not itself remove the independent prohibition of bishul basar b’chalav. Here the proposed heter concerns the **stale absorbed taste**, נותן טעם לפגם.

**Shulchan Arukh YD 122:2** states the ordinary rule: even an eino ben-yomo pot may not be used l’chatchilah for the opposite food, **“גזירה אטו בת יומא”**—a decree because of a pot used within the day. The question is whether that eating-related restriction establishes a prohibition of cooking specifically for a non-Jew. [YD 122:1–2](https://www.sefaria.org/Shulchan_Arukh%2C_Yoreh_De%27ah.122.1-2)

**Pitchei Teshuvah YD 87:8**, citing **Chamudei Daniel §18**, addresses this exact case. It presents permission: where the prohibition is only rabbinic, cooking and benefit were not prohibited; nor is the pot’s dairy status visible, creating mar’it ayin. **But the passage continues “מ״מ יש להחמיר”**—nevertheless one should be stringent—drawing on Rema 87:6 concerning stoking the fire under a non-Jew’s pot. It suggests that the absorption’s origin in actual milk may explain this stringency. Stopping at the opening heter would misrepresent the passage. [Pitchei Teshuvah 87:8](https://www.sefaria.org/Pitchei_Teshuva_on_Shulchan_Arukh%2C_Yoreh_De%27ah.87.8), [Rema, YD 87:6](https://www.sefaria.org/Shulchan_Arukh%2C_Yoreh_De%27ah.87.6)

There is an explicit later permissive ruling: **Kitzur Shulchan Arukh Yalkut Yosef, YD 87, paragraph 30**, permits cooking for a non-Jew in this eino ben-yomo case l’chatchilah, explaining that the decree was not extended to this cooking prohibition. It explicitly distinguishes cooking for Jewish consumption, which remains prohibited l’chatchilah. Thus **one following Yalkut Yosef may cook under these assumptions; one following the caution recorded by Pitchei Teshuvah should refrain l’chatchilah**. The exam answer must preserve this disagreement. [Yalkut Yosef, 87:30, citing Issur VeHeter III, p. 191](https://www.toratemetfreeware.com/online/f_01355_part_41.html)
