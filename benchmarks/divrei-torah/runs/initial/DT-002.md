In Avos, Antignos tells us to serve Hashem “shelo al menas lekabel pras,” without making reward the condition of our service. Notice what remains the same on both sides of the Mishnah: we are avadim serving the Rav. What, then, sustains that avodah when schar is no longer its purpose?

The Rambam gives a precise answer in Hilchos Teshuvah, perek ten, halachah two: “Oseh ha’emes mipnei shehu emes”—a person acts on the truth because it is true. Then he adds, “Vesof hatovah lavo biglalah”: the good will ultimately follow from it.

That addition matters. A person serving me’ahavah still knows that good will come. The change is in what moves him to act. The promised good can remain a consequence without becoming his reason. This helps us hear the Mishnah’s exact words: “shelo al menas.” Our avodah need not depend on what it will bring us.

But “because it is true” could sound impersonal. In halachah five, the Rambam makes the relationship explicit. Learning lishmah means learning because of love for “Adon kol ha’aretz shetzivah bah”—the Master of the whole earth, Who commanded it. Read together, these halachos explain the Mishnah’s eved: he recognizes that the One he serves is worthy of his service. The mitzvah itself has a claim on him, even before he asks what he will gain.

And now halachah five raises a practical difficulty. The Rambam says to keep learning even shelo lishmah. He even describes beginning education through fear and reward. How does that prepare someone for the Mishnah’s avodah?

The continuation is crucial. As understanding develops, we reveal this teaching “me’at me’at,” little by little, and accustom the learner to it gently. The Rambam describes deliberate teaching and habituation. Saying “mitoch shelo lishmah” does not discharge the educator’s responsibility to cultivate a different reason for learning.

Perhaps that gives us an avodah in our own seder. Keep the seder, even when the motives are imperfect. Within it, choose one small act of ameilus—a difficult line we would otherwise leave—and consciously do it out of love for Hashem, Who commanded this learning.

We need not claim that our whole seder has reached the Rambam’s great madreigah. We can begin practicing the change he describes: allowing the mitzvah, and the One Who commanded it, to become our reason for continuing.
