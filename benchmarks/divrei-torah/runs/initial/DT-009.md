Your basic idea works: Avraham’s hospitality teaches us how to practise kindness. Here is a version for roughly two minutes.

Avraham sees three visitors and offers them “pat lechem”—a little bread. Then look at what happens: he hurries to Sarah to arrange the baking, runs to choose a calf, and serves his guests a meal. His actions are much bigger than his offer.

The Gemara in Bava Metzia notices that difference. Rabbi Elazar says: “Tzaddikim omrim me’at ve’osim harbeh”—righteous people say little and do much.

Why does the Gemara draw our attention to what Avraham said? Surely feeding the guests is what matters.

Perhaps the comparison helps us examine a familiar habit. We can feel generous as soon as we have made a generous offer. “Of course I’ll help.” “You’re always welcome.” Saying it feels good. But the other person still needs us to follow through. With Avraham, the offer is only the beginning. The Torah takes us through the work that comes afterward, until the food is in front of his guests.

Think about someone new at school. We might say, “You can sit with us.” That is a good start. Then we can move our bags, make room, introduce them to the others, and bring them into the conversation. Each action gives substance to the welcome. The person who was standing awkwardly with a tray now has a place at the table.

That is one way we can practise Avraham’s chesed. This week, notice one offer of kindness you make. Follow it through, and look for one further thing that would help the person feel welcome. Let the kindness they receive be greater than the kindness you announced.

Sources: [Bereishis 18:2–8](https://www.sefaria.org/Genesis.18.2-8); [Bava Metzia 87a:2](https://www.sefaria.org/Bava_Metzia.87a.2), Rabbi Elazar. The brief translations are my own; the school example is an application of the sources.
