“Vayivaser Yaakov levado”—Yaakov remained alone. Rashi explains that he had forgotten small vessels and returned for them. Rashi in Chullin adds an important detail: the valuable belongings and livestock had already crossed the Yabbok. The things still waiting were the little vessels.

Rabbi Elazar derives from this that tzaddikim cherish their possessions more than their bodies. The Gemara itself asks why, and answers: “Lefi she’ein poshtin yedeihen begezel”—because they do not reach out to take what belongs to others.

How does refusing gezel explain such care over a few vessels? We might have expected the Gemara to explain what a tzaddik does with his money. Instead, it explains how he refuses to acquire it.

Perhaps the point is that honest ownership comes with limits. A person who refuses gezel cannot make himself whole at somebody else’s expense. Replacing a possession must also happen honestly, with whatever effort that requires. He therefore takes seriously what is already his, even when its price is small. The Gemara grounds this attachment in restraint about acquiring money.

The surrounding pesukim help us understand the distinction. This same Yaakov had just sent hundreds of animals to Esav. He explains his purpose: to win Esav’s goodwill. Later, he presses Esav to accept the gift. Yaakov can part with a great deal when the expenditure accomplishes something. Recovering small vessels fits that picture: a deliberate expenditure and a needless loss are different, even when the expenditure is much larger.

There is also a limit within the sugya itself. Immediately afterward, Rabbi Yitzchak learns that a talmid chacham should not go out alone at night. Rashi explains: Yaakov was left alone and was injured. We cannot quote the praise of cherishing possessions and erase the warning beside it. The passage gives us no license to make danger into an ideal.

For us, this suggests a searching question about our own carefulness with money. The Gemara’s explanation begins with keeping our hands away from somebody else’s property. If I am particular about every dollar owed to me, but casual about a small amount I owe someone else, my carefulness has lost the very foundation the Gemara supplies. Yaakov’s small vessels teach a care for possessions rooted in the limits of honest ownership.
